## Preface (pp. v–x)

- **Speed says:** he offers no tricks for particular subjects: "No good can come of such methods, for there are no short cuts to excellence." (p. v)
- **Speed says:** the maker must know his aim: "To have a clear idea of what it is you wish to do, is the first necessity of any successful performance." (p. vii)
- **Speed says:** line and mass should be studied as "two opposite points of view" (p. x), separately, to avoid confusion.
- **Our reading:** this is the spine of the book. Contour and tone-shape are two modes with different primitives.
- **For the app:** method, not subject recipes; every drawing starts with a stated intent and a chosen mode.

## I Introduction (pp. 17–30)

- **Speed says:** training perfects the means, and feeling lies beyond teaching: "Great things are only done in art when the creative instinct of the artist has a well-organised executive faculty at its disposal." (p. 18)
- **Speed says:** "The same fact accurately portrayed by a number of artistic intelligences should be different in each case" (p. 20).
- **Speed says:** he defines art as "the Rhythmic expression of Feeling" (p. 27).
- **For the app:** no likeness-to-reference score for artistic work. An ordering pass (sizes, intervals, hierarchy) is where software can help.

## II Drawing (pp. 31–37)

- **Speed says:** "By drawing is here meant the expression of form upon a plane surface." (p. 31) Form comes before colour.
- **Speed says:** "To express form one must first be moved by it." (p. 31)
- **Speed says:** school studies differ: "In these school studies feeling need not be considered, but only a cold accuracy." (p. 34)
- **Speed says:** artistic accuracy is relative: "Its accuracy depends on the completeness with which it conveys the particular emotional significance that is the object of the drawing." (p. 35) He also keeps "a rough physical standard of rightness" (p. 36).
- **Our reading:** there are two quality bars. Cold accuracy can be measured; expressive rightness belongs to the artist.
- **For the app:** a non-destructive grey view, measured accuracy drills, and checks that report deviations without correcting them.

## III Vision (pp. 38–49)

- **Speed says:** he names "the first difficulty the student of painting has to face: the difficulty of learning to see." (p. 38) The retinal picture is flat, made of masses with "many varieties of edges" (p. 39).
- **Speed says:** a child's first drawing records touch, not sight. Diagram I's "B" shows that a few dark shapes read as a face. "A blind man would not draw differently, could he but see to draw." (p. 44)
- **Speed says:** "Finish in art has no connection with the amount of detail in a picture" (p. 46).
- **Speed says:** looking upside down is "a way of seeing without the deadening influence of touch associations" (p. 47).
- **For the app:** inverted and reduced renders. "Done" means the intent is realised, not that a detail count is reached.

## IV Line Drawing (pp. 50–57)

- **Speed says:** "Outlines can only be said to exist in appearances as the boundaries of masses." (p. 50) Those boundaries are lost and found again.
- **Speed says:** a line directs the eye along its course, and there is "a music of line" (pp. 50–51).
- **Speed says:** "The accumulation of the details of visual observation in art is liable eventually to obscure the main idea and disturb the large sense of design" (p. 54).
- **Speed says:** the Parthenon's departures from the straight are "of the very slightest" (p. 56). A sublime work needs "a carefully wrought and simple line scheme" (p. 57).
- **For the app:** width profiles for accents and lost edges, a direction readout, and a skeleton to check again after detail.

## V Mass Drawing (pp. 58–67)

- **Speed says:** "The reducing of a complicated appearance to a few simple masses is the first necessity of the painter." (pp. 58–59) Plate I shows the stages: lines, a middle ground with a few planes, subdivision, then accents.
- **Speed says:** of Las Meninas: "The focus is nowhere sharp, as this would disturb the contemplation of the large visual impression." (p. 60)
- **Speed says:** silhouette observation "is, of course, the only accurate way in which to observe visual shapes." (p. 65)
- **Speed says:** Degas offers "a consideration of the flat pattern of shapes or masses with a play of lost-and-foundness on the edges" (p. 67).
- **For the app:** a value-group view and mass count, a silhouette comparison, and honest workarounds for lost edges.

## VI The Academic and Conventional (pp. 68–79)

- **Speed says:** "dither" is the least play on accuracy that allows life: "There must be the smallest amount of play that will allow of its working." (p. 71)
- **Speed says:** "it is only the academic that can be taught." (p. 73) But "A drawing is not necessarily academic because it is thorough, but only because it is dead." (p. 73)
- **Speed says:** "naturalism is just as much a convention as any of the other isms" (p. 74). The great artists keep to what their tool can express (p. 78).
- **For the app:** no random jitter sold as "life". Measurement stays with proportion, value, line and silhouette, and vector is treated as its own medium.

## VII The Study of Drawing (pp. 80–83)

- **Speed says:** "it is advisable for the student to study both separately, for there are different things to be learnt" (p. 80).
- **Speed says:** begin at the extremes, bare outline and tone masses, and let them converge (p. 83).
- **Speed says:** the beginner, "timid of losing his outlines, he fears to put down a mass" (p. 83).
- **Speed says:** a Plate XVI caption points to "the flatnesses that give strength to the forms."
- **Our reading:** outlining every object with a single fill is the LLM-SVG failure Speed describes.
- **For the app:** a pure line pass and a pure mass pass, and curvature readouts that show flats inside curves.

## VIII Line Drawing: Practical (pp. 84–109)

- **Speed says:** "appearances must be reduced to terms of a flat surface before they can be expressed on paper." (p. 84)
- **Speed says:** measure against a unit: "It is never advisable to compare other than vertical and horizontal measurements." (p. 88)
- **Speed says:** block in with straight lines, since "The inclination and length of straight lines can be observed with certainty." (p. 92) The block-in "is only a scaffolding, and should always be regarded as such and kicked away" (p. 92).
- **Speed says:** for a curve, "First note the points at which the curvature begins and ends, and then the distances it travels from a line joining these two points" (p. 91).
- **Speed says:** "In Line Drawing shading should only be used to aid the expression of form." (p. 93) Parallel by default; and "lines of shading drawn across the forms suggest softness, lines drawn in curves fulness of form, lines drawn down the forms hardness" (p. 102).
- **For the app:** plumb and unit measures, a block-in on a non-printing layer, inflection readouts, and parallel hatching at one shared angle unless a form calls for another.

## IX Mass Drawing: Practical (pp. 110–126)

- **Speed says:** "Most objects can be reduced broadly into three tone masses, the lights (including the high lights), the half tones, and the shadows." (p. 110)
- **Speed says:** paint into a ground matched to the half tones: "Extreme care should be taken in matching this tone." (p. 111)
- **Speed says:** "Note carefully where the light masses come sharply against the half tones and where they merge softly into them." (p. 112)
- **Speed says:** judge from a distance, with half-closed eyes, or in a black glass: "Faults that are not apparent when close, are easily seen at a little distance." (p. 112)
- **Speed says:** "Nothing is so characteristic of bad modelling as 'gross roundnesses.'" (p. 122)
- **For the app:** a three-value plan on a half-tone ground, planes rather than radial "balls", reduced review views, and deleting and redrawing.

## X Rhythm (pp. 127–136)

- **Speed says:** "There is, as it were, an abstract music of line, tone, and colour." (p. 127) He offers the theory tentatively.
- **Speed says:** unity and variety are the two axes: "Without variety there can be no life." (p. 132) But "variety must always be under the moral control of unity, or it will get out of hand and become extravagant." (p. 134)
- **Speed says:** "don't for one moment think that anything in the nature of rules is going to take the place of the initial artistic impulse" (p. 130).
- **Our reading:** the abstract layout is a design layer of its own, and principles are diagnostics, not generators.
- **For the app:** separate line and mass views. Analysis output is worded as observation, never as a score.

## XI Rhythm: Variety of Line (pp. 137–143)

- **Speed says:** "Line rhythm or music depends on the shape of your lines, their relation to each other and their relation to the boundaries of your panel." (p. 137)
- **Speed says:** the circle "is of all curves the most perfect example of static unity." (p. 138) The egg is livelier: "at no point is its curving at the same ratio as at any other point" (pp. 138–139).
- **Speed says:** on a roughly symmetrical limb, a convex curve faces a concave one (p. 141).
- **Speed says:** "A line of equal thickness is a very dead and inexpressive thing compared with one varied and stressed at certain points." (p. 143)
- **For the app:** curvature readouts per path, width profiles for accents, and no mirroring of organic forms.

## XII Rhythm: Unity of Line (pp. 144–184)

- **Speed says:** "This parallelism can only be used successfully with the simplest lines, such as a straight line or a simple curve" (p. 145).
- **Speed says:** the horizontal goes with calm and the vertical with elevation (p. 149). "The combination of the vertical with the horizontal produces one of the strongest and most arresting chords that you can make" (pp. 155–156).
- **Speed says:** "There is one condition in a composition, that is laid down before you begin, and that is the shape of your panel or canvas." (p. 159)
- **Speed says:** "It is this echoing of lines through a composition that unites the different parts and gives unity to the whole." (p. 172)
- **Speed says:** "the smaller the picture the more detail it should contain, and the larger the less" (p. 183).
- **For the app:** direction and frame readouts, flip views, the artboard fixed first, and detail planned against the viewing size. The emotional meanings stay opinion.

## XIII Rhythm: Variety of Mass (pp. 185–199)

- **Speed says:** masses vary in "their shape, their tone values, their edges, in texture or quality, and in gradation" (p. 185). And "Variety of shape is one of the most difficult things to invent, and one of the commonest things in nature." (p. 185)
- **Speed says:** "Colour only matters in so far as it affects the position of the mass in this imagined scale of black and white." (p. 187)
- **Speed says:** "A monotonous sharpness of edge is hard, stern, and unsympathetic." (p. 192)
- **Speed says:** "a picture that is a catalogue of many little parts separately focussed will not hang together as one visual impression." (p. 195)
- **For the app:** an L* value view and pet-form checks. Vector's default hard edges everywhere are what he calls monotonous.

## XIV Rhythm: Unity of Mass (pp. 200–218)

- **Speed says:** "Nature is the great teacher here, her tone arrangements always possessing unity." (p. 200)
- **Speed says:** "the comparison should be made between nature seen in the glass and your work seen also in the glass." (p. 202)
- **Speed says:** "there should never be pure white or pure black masses in a picture." (pp. 202–203) Then he adds: "But perhaps this is dogmatising" (p. 203).
- **Speed says:** "see to it that in planning your picture you get a good basic structure of simply designed, and as far as possible flat, tones." (p. 207)
- **Speed says:** "a picture obviously composed is badly composed." (p. 218)
- **For the app:** the same filter on reference and work, a flat-tone block-in first, a compressed default value range, and hidden construction.

## XV Rhythm: Balance (pp. 219–226)

- **Speed says:** "Were the balance between them perfect, the result would be dull and dead." (p. 219)
- **Speed says:** bias straight against curved toward the straight, and flat against gradated toward the flat (p. 221). A muddled picture "can be got on the right road again by reducing it to a basis of flat tones" (p. 221).
- **Speed says:** "When the highest lights are low in tone, the darkest darks should be high in tone." (p. 222) It is the extremes that balance, not the amounts.
- **Speed says:** interest can balance mass: "Without the little figure the composition would be out of balance." (p. 225)
- **For the app:** balance readouts are a spectrum, never a target; geometry cannot read interest.

## XVI Rhythm: Proportion (pp. 227–238)

- **Speed says:** a canon is useful only as "offering some standard from which he can depart at the dictates of his artistic instinct." (p. 227)
- **Speed says:** "Unity makes for sublimity, while variety makes for the expression of life." (p. 229) He presents it as his own feeling (p. 228).
- **Speed says:** "Nature seems to abhor equalities, never making two things alike or the same proportion if she can help it." (p. 229)
- **Speed says:** of roughly 5:8, "In such a proportion the eye sees no mathematical relationship." (p. 237)
- **For the app:** no snapping to canons, notes on accidental equal spacing, and layout variants seen one at a time.

## XVII Portrait Drawing (pp. 239–255)

- **Speed says:** "The individual likeness is not in this direction but at the opposite pole to it." (p. 240) That is, it lies away from the common type.
- **Speed says:** likeness is "a sort of chord of which the features will be but the component elements." (p. 241)
- **Speed says:** "The tendency of a tired eye is to see less differentiation, and to hark back to a dull uniformity" (p. 241). And: "it is impossible to lay down hard and fast rules for these things." (p. 242)
- **Speed says:** "the impression of the eyes seen as part of a vivid impression of the head is seldom that they are the same size." (p. 249)
- **For the app:** never average, beautify or mirror a face. Landmarks are placed by the user. Never infer character.

## XVIII The Visual Memory (pp. 256–264)

- **Speed says:** "The memory is the great storehouse of artistic material" (p. 256).
- **Speed says:** "Memory has this great advantage over direct vision: it retains more vividly the essential things" (p. 257).
- **Speed says:** "What the painter has to do is to fix them vividly in his memory, to snapshot them, as it were" (p. 258).
- **Speed says:** "the scene must be committed to memory in terms of whatever medium you intend to employ for reproducing it" (p. 260). "Always work to a scheme on which you have decided" (p. 262).
- **For the app:** a practice workflow for people, never a memory score, and plans written in vector terms before drawing.

## XIX Procedure (pp. 265–270)

- **Speed says:** "It is seldom if ever that an artist puts on paper anything better than he has in his mind before he starts" (p. 265).
- **Speed says:** "let your drawing be an expression of truth perceived intelligently." (p. 266)
- **Speed says:** "Stop and rub out your work until an earlier stage that was right is reached" (p. 267). In expressive drawing, though, "If a wrong line is made, it is left in by the side of the right one in the drawing of many of the masters." (p. 268)
- **Speed says:** "Try and express yourself in as simple, not as complicated a manner as possible. Let every touch mean something" (p. 269).
- **For the app:** plan first, big lines first, revert to the last right state, no filler.

## XX Materials (pp. 271–285)

- **Speed says:** the draughtsman must "be careful never to attempt more than it is capable of doing." (p. 271) And "Art is not a substitute for nature" (p. 272).
- **Speed says:** "They did not attempt anything with a point that a point was not capable of expressing." (p. 273)
- **Speed says:** "There is something that makes for power in the limitations your materials impose." (p. 273)
- **Speed says:** on toned paper, "the half tone of the paper should always be between them." (p. 280) With pen, "No hugging of half tones is possible, things must be reduced to a statement of clear darks" (p. 283).
- **For the app:** vector is a medium, so design to its strengths, map each medium to vector means, and declare any raster simulation.

## XXI Conclusion (pp. 286–288)

- **Speed says:** "The camera is a convenience to be avoided by the student." (p. 286)
- **Speed says:** "The variations on mechanical accuracy in the work of a fine draughtsman need not be, and seldom are, conscious variations." (p. 287)
- **Speed says:** "The 'seeking after originality' so much advocated would be better put 'seeking for sincerity.'" (p. 287) And "Good style depends on a clear idea of what it is you wish to do" (p. 288).
- **For the app:** say when output was traced. Never fake vitality with jitter. The product claims no originality.

## Appendix: the Phi proportion (pp. 289–291)

- **Speed says:** the additive series 5, 8, 13, 21 … converges on one ratio: "There is only one proportion that will do this" (p. 289).
- **Speed says:** he finds φ in seven pictures, and concludes "the eye evidently takes some especial pleasure in it, whatever may eventually be found to be the physiological reason underlying it." (p. 291)
- **Our reading:** the mathematics is right. The aesthetic test is post hoc, with no tolerance given.
- **For the app:** φ at most as an optional, non-snapping guide labelled historical. Never a score.

## Author's own warnings against formula

Speed's own statements, gathered from the four ledgers:

- No short cuts or "wrinkles" (p. v). Selection is "too subtle to be tabulated" (p. 32).
- Followers who copy a method lose its life: Michelangelo's (p. 56), and Degas's, who "unintelligently copy his method" (p. 67).
- Measurements "are never to be taken as absolutely accurate" (p. 88). Blocking in is scaffolding to be "kicked away" (p. 92).
- His methods are one way among many: "a student of any originality will find one that suits his temperament" (p. 100); "I don't suggest that this is the right or only way of painting" (p. 112). Brush hatching is "so mechanical a method" (p. 118).
- Rules never replace "the initial artistic impulse" (p. 130). Canons and composite ideal types produce deadness (pp. 133–134).
- The pure white and black rule "perhaps … is dogmatising" (p. 203). A composition "perfect as far as any rules … go" may be "of no account whatever" (p. 216). "a picture obviously composed is badly composed" (p. 218).
- Perfect balance is "dull and dead" (p. 219). Canons "have not been a great success" (p. 227). For portraits "it is impossible to lay down hard and fast rules" (p. 242).
- He rejects "the camera idea of truth" (p. 266) and "a mechanical standard of accuracy" (p. 286). Yet he licenses no carelessness: precision comes first, for years (pp. 286–287).

**For the app:** describe, never score. Metrics belong to drills and physical plausibility; intent, selection and character stay with the artist.

## 1913 claims that are artistic opinion, not verified science

From the ledgers' science caveats. None of these may be stated as fact in product text.

- **A shared, innate sense of harmony** (pp. 21, 27). Empirical aesthetics finds large cultural and individual variation.
- **Touch as the root of outline and of seeing solidity** (pp. 40–44, 50). It is his hypothesis. Inversion works for other reasons, such as the face inversion effect.
- **Binocular vision and focusing as the "principal" depth cues** (p. 40). Monocular cues matter at least as much.
- **Where the eye goes:** lines leading it (pp. 50, 160), corners and light masses attracting it (pp. 160, 201), and narrated eye paths (p. 236). These are heuristics at best.
- **Fixed emotional meanings of lines and tones:** horizontal calm, vertical elevation, right-angle drama, jagged terror (pp. 149–189). These are convention.
- **Beauty as conformity to type** (pp. 134–135) **and physiognomy** (pp. 244–245). Never to become scoring of people; modern science does not support physiognomy.
- **Memory "retains more vividly the essential things"** (p. 257). Memory is reconstructive and normalising.
- **The golden section as something the eye "evidently" enjoys** (p. 291). The evidence is weak, and his test was post hoc.
- **Bodily empathy with form** (pp. 32–34). It reflects the *Einfühlung* aesthetics of around 1900.
- **The "Eastern mind"** (p. 59). A period stereotype.
- **Colour:** warm and cold as halves of the spectrum (p. 223), and shadows warm when lights are cool (p. 114). Painters' conventions.
- **Photography lacking fine form, and illusion opposed to life** (pp. 31–32, 74). Period taste.
- **Art history:** light and shade "not seriously perceived until Leonardo" (p. 51), and Greek conic against Roman circular mouldings (p. 134). Overstated or unsupported.
- **Attributions to verify before reuse:**
  - zinc white for Holbein (p. 279);
  - "The style is the man" credited to Flaubert (p. 288; usually attributed to Buffon);
  - passing expressions "very similar in most people" (p. 253), which is contested.
